Payment models are requirements

Care-management platforms fail when they are designed from the clinical workflow forward and the billing rules backward. Design from both at once.

New payment models arrive with a clinical intent and a rulebook. The intent is usually admirable: pay for the coordination, monitoring and caregiver support that fee-for-service never rewarded. The rulebook is where programs succeed or fail, because it specifies exactly which patients qualify, which activities count, how often they must occur, what must be documented and what the organization will be paid. A care-management program that is clinically excellent and operationally unable to prove it did the work is not a program; it is a cost center.

Most platforms get this wrong in one of two directions. Some are built around the clinical workflow with billing added later, which produces beautiful care plans and unreconciled revenue. Others are built around the codes, which produces compliant documentation and clinicians who feel they are working for the billing system. The answer is to treat the payment model as a first-class source of requirements alongside the clinical model, and to design the platform so that doing the right clinical thing automatically produces the right operational record.

What the rulebook tells the architect

Read carefully, a program's rules specify most of the platform. Eligibility criteria define the enrollment and screening logic. Assessment and tiering requirements define the data model for patient status. Contact cadences and activity definitions define the task engine and the documentation templates. Monitoring requirements define the device integrations. Consent and caregiver provisions define the identity and access model. Reporting and reconciliation requirements define the data warehouse. The architecture is, to a large degree, a derivation.

One platform, many programs

The organizations that will do best over the next several years are those able to participate in several programs at once: advanced primary care management, dementia and cognitive care, behavioral health integration, state transformation models, remote monitoring. These share most of their machinery and differ in rules. A platform with a stable core and a program-specific extension pack for each model lets the organization add programs without rebuilding, and lets the revenue model be stacked rather than chosen. That is an architecture decision, and it should be made before the first program launches, not after the third.

Takeaway. Derive the platform from the clinical model and the rulebook together. Build a stable core with program-specific extension packs so new models plug in instead of starting over.
